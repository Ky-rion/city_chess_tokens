### 1. The Particle Zoo: Building Blocks of the Universe

- **Quarks:** These interact via the strong force and make up protons and neutrons. They come in pairs: up/down (u,d), charm/strange (c,s), and top/bottom (t,b).
    
- **Leptons:** These do not interact via the strong force. The most famous is the electron (e), but there are heavier versions called the muon (μ) and the tau (τ). Each of these has a corresponding, very light, neutral particle called a neutrino (ν).

### 3. Exam Goldmine: The "Open Questions"

Despite its success, the Standard Model is incomplete. It fails to explain several massive mysteries of our universe:

- **Matter-Antimatter Asymmetry:** Why is there so much more matter than antimatter in the universe?.
    
- **Dark Matter and Dark Energy:** The SM only describes normal matter, which makes up a tiny fraction of the universe.
    
- **Neutrino Masses:** The original SM predicted neutrinos to be completely massless, but we now know they have a tiny mass.
    
- **The Hierarchy Problem & Vacuum Stability:** These involve complex quantum math regarding why gravity is so weak compared to other forces, and whether the foundational energy state of our universe is truly stable (which depends heavily on the exact masses of the top quark and the Higgs boson).

- **Gravity:** The SM completely leaves out gravity.
    

To solve these, physicists are proposing new ideas like Supersymmetry, String Theory, Axions, and Leptoquarks.

### 4. The Hunt for "New Physics"

Because we know the SM is incomplete, physicists are trying to "break" it to find new physics. Your notes outline two main experimental strategies to do this:

- **Direct Searches:** Smashing particles together at incredibly high energies to create brand-new, undiscovered particles (like a hypothetical Z′ boson) directly in the collision.

- **Indirect Searches:** Looking at the decay of known particles. If undiscovered, "virtual" particles exist, they can subtly alter the decay rates or couplings of standard particles in ways the SM doesn't predict. For example, researchers look at very specific mathematical ratios, like R(D) and R(D∗), in the decay of B mesons to test if lepton flavor universality is being violated.
