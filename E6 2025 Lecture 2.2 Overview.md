### 1. The Golden Rule of Crystallography

To understand any crystal, you must memorize this fundamental definition:

**Crystal Structure = Lattice + Basis**

- **Lattice (Gitter):** This is a purely mathematical concept—a periodic, infinite arrangement of points in space.
    
- **Basis:** This is the physical reality. It is the structural unit (an atom, a group of atoms, or a molecule) that you attach to every single lattice point to build the actual crystal.
    

### 2. Bravais Lattices

Auguste Bravais figured out that there are only a limited number of ways to arrange points periodically in space.

- Mathematically, a 3D Bravais lattice is defined by the translation vector: $\vec{R}=n_{1}\vec{a}_{1}+n_{2}\vec{a}_{2}+n_{3}\vec{a}_{3}$.
    
- The vectors $\vec{a}_{i}$ are the primitive lattice vectors, and their lengths are the lattice constants.
    
- In 2D, there are 5 basic Bravais lattices (square, rectangular, oblique, rectangular centered, and hexagonal).
    
- In 3D, there are exactly 14 Bravais lattices, which are categorized into 7 crystal systems (such as cubic, tetragonal, and hexagonal). * **Exam Tip:** You should know the concept of a **Wigner-Seitz cell**. It is a special primitive unit cell that contains exactly one lattice point right in its center, constructed by drawing perpendicular bisectors to all nearest-neighbor lattice points.
    

### 3. Key Crystal Structures and Packing Density

For your exam, you will likely need to calculate or state the "Packing Density" (Packungsdichte). This is the fraction of the total volume of the unit cell that is actually filled by atoms (assuming atoms are hard spheres touching their nearest neighbors).

Here are the heavy hitters you need to memorize:

- **Simple Cubic (sc):** Atoms are only at the 8 corners of the cube. Because each corner is shared by 8 adjacent cubes, there is only $8 \cdot \frac{1}{8} = 1$ atom per unit cell. It is very inefficient, filling only about 52% of the space. Polonium is the only element that naturally takes this form.
    
- **Body-Centered Cubic (bcc):** Atoms are at the 8 corners, plus 1 atom right in the center. This yields 2 atoms per unit cell and a packing density of approximately 0.68. Metals like Iron, Chromium, and Tungsten form this way.
    
- **Face-Centered Cubic (fcc):** Atoms are at the 8 corners, plus 1 atom in the center of each of the 6 faces. This gives 4 atoms per unit cell. It achieves the maximum possible packing density of about 0.74. Aluminum, Gold, and Silver use this structure. * **Hexagonal Close-Packed (hcp):** This structure also achieves the maximum packing density of roughly 0.74, but it has a different layer arrangement.
    

**The Stacking Sequence (Stapelfolge):** Both fcc and hcp are optimally packed, but they differ in how their layers stack.

- **hcp** stacks in an ABABAB... pattern.
    
- **fcc** stacks in an ABCABC... pattern.
    

### 4. Important Compound Structures

Sometimes the basis contains more than one atom.

- **CsCl Structure:** This is a simple cubic lattice but with a 2-atom basis. The cation is surrounded by 8 anions (and vice versa).
    
- **NaCl Structure:** This is an fcc lattice with a 2-atom basis. Each cation is surrounded by 6 anions.
    
- **Diamond & Zincblende:** Both are based on an fcc lattice with a 2-atom basis, resulting in a tetrahedral structure where each atom binds to 4 others. If both atoms in the basis are identical (like Carbon or Silicon), it is the Diamond structure. If they are different (like ZnS or GaAs), it is the Zincblende structure.
    

### 5. Miller Indices (Navigating the Crystal)

You will undoubtedly be asked to identify lattice planes and directions using Miller Indices. * **To find the $(hkl)$ index for a plane:** 1. Find where the plane intersects the $x$, $y$, and $z$ axes in terms of lattice constants. 2. Take the reciprocal (inverse) of those numbers. 3. Multiply by the lowest common denominator to get the smallest integers.

- **Notation matters:** $(hkl)$ refers to a specific plane, while $\{hkl\}$ refers to a whole family of symmetrically equivalent planes. Directions are written as $[uvw]$ and families of directions as $\langle uvw \rangle$.
    
- **Lattice Spacing (Netzebenenabstand):** For orthogonal crystal systems (like cubic), the distance $d_{hkl}$ between parallel $(hkl)$ planes is calculated as:
    
    $\frac{1}{d_{hkl}} = \sqrt{(\frac{h}{a})^{2}+(\frac{k}{b})^{2}+(\frac{l}{c})^{2}}$
