### 2. What Defines a Semiconductor? (Exam Focus)

You will absolutely need to know how to define a semiconductor based on its bandgap energy ($E_{g}$) and its temperature dependence.

**Classification by Bandgap:**

- **Metals:** $E_{g} = 0$.
    
- **Semiconductors:** $0 < E_{g} < 4$ eV.
    
- **Insulators:** $E_{g} > 4$ eV.
    

**Temperature Dependence:**

- At an absolute temperature of $T = 0$ K, all electrons sit below the Fermi energy ($E_{F}$).
    
- Because no electrons can jump the bandgap at 0 K, the specific resistivity ($\rho$) approaches infinity ($\rho \rightarrow \infty$).
    
- However, at temperatures above 0 K, the resistivity decreases.
    
- This happens because thermal energy generates charge carriers, allowing the material to conduct electricity.

### 3. Types of Semiconductors

Semiconductors can be classified in several different ways based on their structure and composition:

- **Intrinsic vs. Doped:** Intrinsic means pure, while doped means the material is mixed with foreign atoms (impurities) to change its properties.
    
- **Crystalline vs. Amorphous:** We focus almost exclusively on crystalline structures in this course.
    
- **Direct vs. Indirect:** This classification is based on their optical transitions.
    
- **Elemental Semiconductors:** These are made from single elements, primarily Group IV elements like Carbon (Graphite), Silicon (Si), Germanium (Ge), and others.

### 4. Manufacturing: The Czochralski Process (1916)

To make high-functioning computer chips, we need incredibly pure elemental semiconductors (purity levels around $1:10^{-9}$). **How it works:**

1. Polycrystalline silicon (99.99999% pure) is melted down.
    
2. A tiny silicon seed crystal is introduced into the melt.
    
3. A single crystal is then slowly pulled out of the melt while rotating.
    
4. The diameter of the resulting crystal ingot is carefully controlled by adjusting the temperature, rotation speed, and pulling speed.
    
5. Finally, these ingots are sliced into wafers that are approximately 300 $\mu$m thick.

### 5. Compound Semiconductors and Alloys (Exam Focus)

Interestingly, you can combine two or more elements to create new compounds that exhibit semiconducting properties. **Compound Semiconductors:**

- **III-V Compounds:** By exchanging an electron from a Group V atom to a Group III atom, you get a material that behaves like an elemental semiconductor.
    
- The ionic character of this bond usually increases the bandgap compared to elemental semiconductors. Examples include GaAs, AlAs, and GaN.
    
- **II-VI Compounds:** These are even more strongly ionic than III-V materials.
    
- Because of this strong ionic character, they typically have even larger bandgaps (with the exception of Mercury-containing compounds). Examples include CdS and ZnO.
    

**Alloys (Legierungen):** You can also create alloys by mixing two semiconductors, such as combining AlAs and GaAs to create $Al_{x}Ga_{1-x}As$ (where $0 \le x \le 1$).

- The properties of the alloy fall between the properties of the individual components.
    
- **Vegard's Rule:** This is a good empirical approximation stating that the lattice constant of the alloy is a linear combination of its parts. For example: $a_{Al_{x}Ga_{1-x}As} = x \cdot a_{AlAs} + (1-x) \cdot a_{GaAs}$.
    
- **Bandgap of Alloys:** Unlike the lattice constant, determining the bandgap often requires a quadratic approach: $E_{g}(Al_{x}Ga_{1-x}As) = (ax^{2} + bx + c)$ eV.
    
- **Prerequisites for Alloys:** To successfully mix them, the individual compound semiconductors must have the same crystal structure, and their lattice constants and ionic radii must be similar.

### 6. Organic Semiconductors

Finally, we have organic materials. Normally, organics are electrical insulators.

- However, if they possess a conjugated bond system made of double bonds, triple bonds, or aromatic rings, they can become electrically conductive.
    
- They are categorized by molar mass into conjugated molecules (like Anthracene) and conjugated polymers (like Polyaniline).
    
- **Pros & Cons:** They are cheap to manufacture, lightweight, and bendable. However, their main problems are stability and durability.
    
- **Applications:** You will find them in OLED displays, organic solar cells, and organic transistors.
