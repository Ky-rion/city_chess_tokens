Welcome back! This is where the physics gets truly exciting. In Part (c), we are looking at the "power" of our microscope. In particle physics, $Q^2$ isn't just a math variable; it determines how small of a detail we can see inside the proton.

Think of it like this: a high-resolution camera needs a very short wavelength of light. In our case, $Q^2$ is related to that wavelength. The higher the $Q^2$, the deeper we can "see" into the proton's structure.

---

### 1. Maximum $Q^2$ at HERA (Collider Scenario)

The question asks for the **maximal** value of $Q^2$. Theoretically, the most "violent" collision possible is a "head-on" elastic backward-scattering event. This is like a tennis ball (the electron) hitting a fast-moving bowling ball (the proton) and being sent flying directly back the way it came.

In this extreme case, we reach the limit where $Q^2$ is equal to the square of the total center-of-mass energy ($s$) divided by $c^2$.

**The Calculation:**

-   From Part (a), we know $s \approx 10^5 \text{ GeV}^2$.
    
-   Therefore, $Q^2_{\text{max}} = s/c^2 \approx 10^5 \text{ (GeV/c)}^2$.
    

This is a massive number, representing the full energy potential of the HERA collider being used to probe a single point.

---

### 2. $Q^2$ for a Fixed-Target Experiment

Now, let's look at the "stationary target" scenario. Imagine we have a beam of electrons at **300 GeV** hitting a proton that is sitting still.

As we learned in Part (a), for a stationary target, the formula for $s$ simplifies because the proton's momentum is zero. Using the same logic for $Q^2_{\text{max}}$:

$$Q^2_{\text{max}} = s/c^2 \approx 2E_e m_p$$

**The Calculation:**

-   $E_e = 300 \text{ GeV}$
    
-   $m_p \approx 0.938 \text{ GeV/c}^2$ (standard proton mass)
    
-   $Q^2_{\text{max}} \approx 2 \cdot 300 \cdot 0.938 \approx \mathbf{560 \text{ (GeV/c)}^2}$
    

**The Comparison:**

Notice the staggering difference! Even with a powerful 300 GeV beam, a fixed-target experiment only reaches a $Q^2$ of **560**, while the HERA collider reaches **100,000**. This is why we need colliders to study the smallest building blocks of matter.

---

### 3. Spatial Resolution: How deep can we see?

This is my favorite part. In physics, there is an inverse relationship between momentum and distance, governed by the uncertainty principle: $\Delta x \approx \hbar/Q$.

A higher $Q$ (the square root of $Q^2$) means a smaller $\Delta x$, which is our **spatial resolution**. It’s the smallest "pixel" size our particle microscope can resolve.
