### 1. The Magic of the Nanoscale & Quantum Confinement

In our everyday experience, a material's properties do not change just because we cut it into smaller pieces. However, when we shrink a semiconductor down to the nanoscale (typically around 2.0 nm to 40 nm), quantum effects become dominant.

The most important concept here is **Quantum Confinement** (Quanteneinschränkungseffekte).

- In a normal 3D bulk semiconductor, an electron-hole pair (called an exciton) moves freely.
    
- As the crystal shrinks and approaches the exciton Bohr radius, the movement of this exciton is severely restricted.
    
- This restriction gives the exciton additional energy, which effectively increases the material's bandgap.
    

For your exam, you should definitely recognize the **Brus Equation**, which is used to estimate the optical bandgap of a spherical nanocrystal (or quantum dot):

$$E_{QD}=E_{g}+\frac{h^{2}}{8r_{QD}^{2}}(\frac{1}{m_{e}^{*}}+\frac{1}{m_{h}^{*}})$$

### 2. Dimensionality Matters

Confinement depends heavily on the dimensionality of the crystal. The more dimensions we restrict, the stronger the quantum confinement effect.

- **3D (Bulk):** No confinement.
    
- **2D (Quantum Well):** Confined in one dimension.
    
- **1D (Quantum Wire):** Confined in two dimensions.
    
- **0D (Quantum Dot):** Confined in all three dimensions.
    

### 3. How We Make Them: Synthesis Methods

There are two primary ways to grow these structures, each with its own trade-offs:

**A. Epitaxy (Epitaktisches Wachstum)**

- **The Process:** Growing the crystal layer by layer on a substrate. Methods include Molecular Beam Epitaxy (MBE), Metalorganic Chemical Vapor Deposition (MOCVD), and Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD).
    
- **Pros & Cons:** It offers atomic precision. However, it comes with high costs, long manufacturing times, and difficulties in upscaling.
    

**B. Colloidal Synthesis (Kolloidalsynthese)**

- **The Process:** We use a "hot-injection method" in an inert gas environment (since materials like Cadmium oxidize in air). Non-metallic precursors are injected into hot metallic precursors to trigger immediate nucleation. The solution cools to freeze the size of the nuclei, and is then reheated for controlled growth. Finally, it is rapidly cooled to set the size and washed.
    
- **Pros & Cons:** It allows for highly precise size control, high material flexibility, and is scalable. The downside is the harsh reaction conditions and the difficulty of rapid cooling on an industrial scale.
    
- _Exam Trivia:_ The discovery and synthesis of quantum dots won the 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (Bawendi, Brus, and Ekimov)!
    

### 4. Fixing the Surface: Ligands and Core/Shell Structures

Because quantum dots are so small, a huge percentage of their atoms (up to 25% for a 5 nm dot) are on the surface. These surface atoms are "undercoordinated" and form trap states with discrete energies that ruin the dot's light emission (non-radiative recombination). We have two ways to fix this:

- **Organic Ligands:** We wrap the inorganic core in an organic shell. Ligands (classified as L-type electron donors, Z-type electron acceptors, or charged X-types) passivate these trap states and strongly increase the quantum yield.
    
- **Core/Shell Heterostructures:** Since organic layers can suppress charge transport, we can instead grow an inorganic shell around the core. For example, wrapping a CdSe core in a ZnS shell works beautifully because they have similar lattice constants but ZnS has a larger bandgap. This locks the exciton inside the core so it cannot "see" the surface traps, resulting in quantum yields up to 99%.
    

### 5. Optoelectronic Quirks

While semiconductor quantum dots are much more stable than standard fluorescent dyes (they show very little photobleaching), they have some unique behavioral quirks:

- **Inhomogeneous Broadening:** In a large batch (ensemble), not all quantum dots are perfectly identical in size, shape, or ligand coverage, causing slightly different emission wavelengths.
    
- **Fluorescence Blinking:** If you look at a _single_ quantum dot, its emission intensity fluctuates on and off. This is caused by charging of the dot and Auger recombination.
    
- **Dark States:** Because of strong confinement, exciton states split into "bright" and "dark" states. Dark states have optically forbidden transitions, which lowers the radiative recombination rate, especially at low temperatures.
    

### 6. Shape Shifting: Nanoplatelets (NPLs)

We are not limited to spheres! Depending on the crystal structure and ligands, we can grow rods, tetrapods, and 2D nanoplatelets.

- **Exam Highlight:** Nanoplatelets are special because they can be grown with exact monolayer precision in thickness. Because their thickness is perfectly uniform, there is almost no inhomogeneous broadening, resulting in extremely narrow emission linewidths. They can also be made into core/shell or core/crown structures.
    

### 7. Why Do We Care? (Applications)

Nanocrystals are incredibly versatile for optical and optoelectronic applications. You can find them used as fluorescent markers in life sciences, in LEDs, in photocatalysis for splitting water, in solar cells, in modern quantum dot (QD) displays, and in field-effect transistors.
