Welcome back! Things are about to get messy—in a good way.

In Part (a) and (b), we played a gentle game of billiards. The electron hit the proton, and the proton bounced away intact. That was **Elastic Scattering**.

Now, we are entering the realm of **Inelastic Scattering**. This is where the electron hits the proton so hard (transferring so much energy) that the proton actually _shatters_. It doesn't just recoil; it breaks apart into new particles.

Let's break down the solution for **Part (c)**.

---

### 1. The Sketch: What changed?

Look at the new Feynman diagram in your solution `image_975cd7.png`.

-   **Top Half (The Electron):**
    
    Notice that the top half of the diagram is identical to the previous one. The electron comes in ($p$), emits a photon ($q$), and bounces off ($p'$). The electron itself is a fundamental particle—it cannot break apart.
    
-   **Bottom Half (The Proton):**
    
    This is where the action is.
    
    -   **Incoming:** You still have a single proton ($P$) coming in.
        
    -   **Outgoing:** Instead of a single line leaving the collision, you see a **spray of multiple lines**. The handwritten note labels this _hadronisches System_ (German for "Hadronic System").
        

**What is a Hadronic System?**

Hadrons are particles made of quarks (like protons, neutrons, and pions). When the proton shatters, the energy creates a shower of these particles. Instead of just tracking one proton leaving, we now have to track this whole "blob" of new particles.

### 2. The Four-Vector of the Hadronic System ($P'$)

The question asks to define the four-vector for this new mess of particles.

In the solution, this is defined as:

$$P' = \left( \frac{E^*}{c}, \vec{P}^* \right)$$

-   **$E^*$**: This is the _total_ energy of all the debris (the shattered pieces) combined.
    
-   **$\vec{P}^*$**: This is the _total_ momentum vector of all the debris combined.
    

**The Physics Principle (Conservation):**

Even though the proton broke into pieces, physics still demands that energy and momentum are conserved. The "blob" of outgoing particles must carry exactly what was put into it.

$$P' = P + q$$

_(The outgoing mess = The original proton + The kick from the photon)_

### 3. The New Variable: Invariant Mass ($W$)

This is the most critical concept in inelastic scattering.

In the handwritten solution, you see a formula at the bottom right:

$$Wc = \sqrt{P'^2} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{E^*}{c}\right)^2 - \vec{P}^{*2}}$$

**What is $W$?**

$W$ represents the **Invariant Mass** of the hadronic system.

-   **In Elastic Scattering (Part a/b):** The outgoing particle was just a proton. Its mass was fixed at $M_p$ (approx $0.938 \text{ GeV}/c^2$).
    
-   **In Inelastic Scattering (Part c):** We have added energy to the system that _didn't_ turn into motion; it turned into **new matter** (new particles). Therefore, the mass of this new system ($W$) is heavier than the original proton.
    

$$W > M_p$$

**Breaking down the formula:**

The student uses the standard relativistic definition of mass: $m^2 = E^2 - p^2$.

So, to find the mass ($W$) of the outgoing system $P'$, they square the four-vector:

$$W^2 c^2 = P' \cdot P'$$

$$W = \frac{1}{c} \sqrt{(P')^2}$$

### Summary

To summarize the answer to Part (c):

1.  **The Diagram:** Shows a single proton entering, but a "fan" or "spray" of multiple particles leaving the collision vertex.
    
2.  **The Vector:** The outgoing system $P'$ is the sum of the incoming proton and the transferred photon ($P' = P + q$).
    
3.  **The Mass:** The system now has a variable mass $W$, which is calculated by finding the "length" of the four-vector $P'$.
