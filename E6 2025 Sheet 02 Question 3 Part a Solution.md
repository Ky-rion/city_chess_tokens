### The Core Concept: It's all just Carbon

First, look at the last line of the solution provided:
> *"Materialien bestehen nur aus Kohlenstoff (Nichtmetall), $\delta EN = 0$, unpolare Bindung"*

Here is the translation and the physics behind it:
*   **Translation:** "Materials consist only of Carbon (non-metal), $\Delta EN = 0$, non-polar bond."
*   **The Explanation:** Both structures in your image are made 100% of Carbon atoms.
    *   **Covalent Bonding:** In chemistry and physics, when two non-metals (like Carbon) meet, they like to *share* electrons rather than steal them. This sharing is called a **Covalent Bond**.
    *   **Non-polar ($\Delta EN = 0$):** "EN" stands for Electronegativity (how hungry an atom is for electrons). Since a Carbon atom is bonding to another Carbon atom, they have the exact same "hunger." Neither side pulls harder. It is a perfect tie. Therefore, the bond is **non-polar**.

So, the foundation for *both* materials is the **strong, non-polar covalent bond**. Now, let's see how they differ.

### 1. Diamond (The Structure on the Left)

**The Solution says:**
> *"Diamant: kovalente Bindungen"*

**The Professor's Explanation:**
Look at the left image. Do you see how every single black dot (Carbon atom) is connected to four other dots? It looks like a rigid 3D cage.

*   **The Bond:** In a diamond, every carbon atom uses all of its available electrons to form strong covalent bonds with four neighbors.
*   **The Geometry:** This creates a **tetrahedral** network.
*   **The Consequence:** Because every atom is holding hands tightly with four others in a giant 3D grid, the structure is incredibly rigid. There are no "weak spots." This is why diamond is the hardest natural material on Earth. To break a diamond, you have to actually snap these strong covalent bonds.

### 2. Graphite (The Structure on the Right)

**The Solution says:**
> *"Graphit: kovalente Bindungen zwischen C-Atomen in Ebene (Graphen-Schichten), schwache van der Waals Kräfte zwischen den Ebenen"*

**The Professor's Explanation:**
This is where it gets really interesting. Look at the right image. It doesn't look like a cage; it looks like a stack of honeycombs or sheets of paper floating on top of each other. Graphite has a "split personality"—it uses **two** different types of forces.

**Type A: The Strong Force (Inside the Sheet)**
*   Inside those flat honeycomb sheets (which we call *Graphene* layers), the carbon atoms are holding on to each other with those same super-strong **covalent bonds** we saw in diamond.
*   However, each carbon only connects to *three* neighbors here, forming hexagons.

**Type B: The Weak Force (Between the Sheets)**
*   Now, look at the dotted lines in the drawing connecting the top layer to the bottom layer. The solution identifies these as **"schwache van der Waals Kräfte"** (weak van der Waals forces).
*   **What are they?** These are not true bonds where electrons are shared. They are essentially weak electrostatic attractions—like static cling or a very weak magnet.
*   **The Consequence:** Because the force *between* the layers is so weak, the layers can slide over each other very easily.
*   **Real World Application:** When you write with a pencil, you aren't scratching the paper. You are pressing down, and the weak van der Waals forces break, leaving stacks of graphite sheets behind on the paper. That is your writing!

If you are asked this on an exam, here is the mental checklist based on the solution:

1.  **Chemical Makeup:** Both are pure Carbon.
